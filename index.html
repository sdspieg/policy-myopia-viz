The structural finding

17 of 21 textbooks name a generation step — the slot exists across all traditions. But only 3–4 give methodological guidance for HOW to generate (Thissen & Walker, Enserink/Delft, Patton/Sawicki/Clark, Nagel — marked ✦). Only Bobrow & Dryzek (✦✦) explicitly draw on multiple intellectual traditions for option design — the closest any textbook comes to cross-domain search. The evaluation side has CBA, MCA, sensitivity testing, robustness analysis, criteria weighting, trade-off matrices — each with its own chapter. Generation gets a checkbox.

What the sequences reveal

Two patterns are invisible in the coverage matrix but jump out here:

1. Criteria-first vs generation-first. Bardach and his followers put generation (gold) before criteria (teal). But Patton/Sawicki/Clark, Weimer & Vining, McLaughlin, and Shalita & Schwartz reverse this: decide what you're looking for, then look. Neither camp acknowledges the other's ordering as a design choice — it is simply assumed.

2. The evaluation tail. Every textbook that has a generation step follows it with 2–4 evaluation/comparison steps (red blocks). The visual weight is unmistakable: a thin gold bar followed by a long red tail. That is the asymmetry the paper measures.

New finding from expanded corpus: Adding 10 textbooks (Howlett/Ramesh/Perl, Kraft/Furlong, Anderson, Mintrom, Pal, Birkland, Mead, Bobrow/Dryzek, Nagel, Stone) reinforces the pattern: 6 more generation slots, only 2 more generation methods (Nagel ✦, Bobrow/Dryzek ✦✦). The cycle-model textbooks (Howlett, Kraft, Anderson, Pal, Birkland) all have a "formulation" stage but treat it as a political process, not an analytical method. Bobrow & Dryzek (1987) is the one partial exception: their design-oriented framework explicitly draws on 5 intellectual traditions (welfare economics, public choice, social structure, information processing, political philosophy) — the closest any textbook comes to cross-domain option generation.

Problem Definition — cross-textbook differences

  • Bardach: "Define the problem" — a brief, client-oriented scoping. Emphasis on keeping it small and actionable.
  • Weimer & Vining: Three sub-steps: receive the problem, delineate it analytically, model it. Much more structured — includes market-failure and government-failure frameworks as diagnostic tools.
  • Dunn: "Problem structuring" — an explicit analytical procedure, not just scoping. Includes boundary analysis, classificational analysis, hierarchy analysis. The most methodologically rich problem step.
  • Enserink/Delft: Three steps: problem formulation + system analysis (causal mapping) + actor analysis (stakeholder mapping). The multi-actor tradition treats problem definition as inherently political.
  • Kingdon: Problem definition is a political process, not an analytical one — problems are "constructed" through indicators, focusing events, and feedback.

The pattern: Problem definition ranges from a 2-page scoping exercise (Bardach) to a multi-chapter analytical framework (Dunn, Enserink). This variation receives far less attention than the evaluation-method variation.

Option Generation — cross-textbook differences

  • Bardach: "Start with the obvious" + "ask practitioners." ~15 pages, mostly cautionary ("don't have too many"). No method.
  • Weimer & Vining: A subsection, not a chapter. Lists sources (existing policy, theoretical models, modified proposals) but no search protocol.
  • Patton, Sawicki & Clark ✦: A full chapter. Named techniques: no-action baseline, quick surveys, literature review, cross-jurisdictional comparison, policy-instrument typologies, brainstorming. The closest to a structured method in the Bardach tradition.
  • Thissen & Walker ✦: Explicitly separates generation from assessment within "solution analysis." Discusses scenario-based option development and design-oriented approaches. The only textbook that treats generation as a methodological problem of comparable dignity to evaluation.
  • Enserink/Delft ✦: Scenarios + option development as a structured phase. The TU Delft tradition gives generation more weight than the Anglo-American mainstream.
  • Everyone else: "Identify alternatives" as a named step with ~0.5–1 chapter. Guidance: brainstorm, consult stakeholders, look at what others did.

The gap: The evaluation side has CBA, MCA, sensitivity analysis, robustness testing — each a named method with its own textbook chapter and sometimes its own course. The generation side has "brainstorm." The step exists; the discipline does not.

The missing method: Zero textbooks describe cross-domain search — systematically looking outside the analyst's home discipline for mechanisms, instruments, or design patterns that bear on the problem but use different vocabulary.

Criteria Selection — cross-textbook differences

  • Sequence disagreement: Bardach, Bromell, and Thissen & Walker put criteria AFTER generation. Patton/Sawicki/Clark, Weimer & Vining, McLaughlin, and Shalita & Schwartz put criteria BEFORE generation. The field does not agree on whether you should know what you're evaluating before you generate options.
  • Bardach: "Select the criteria" — efficiency, equity, liberty, political feasibility. Presented as a menu to choose from.
  • Weimer & Vining: "Goals and constraints" — framed as the client's objectives plus legal/political/administrative constraints. More explicitly client-oriented than Bardach.
  • Dunn: Criteria are embedded in the "Recommendation" procedure rather than a separate step. Discusses optimization, satisficing, and mixed scanning as decision rules.
  • Enserink/Delft: Criteria emerge from the actor analysis — different stakeholders have different criteria, and the framework makes this explicit.

Why the order matters for policy myopia: If you set criteria BEFORE generating options, your criteria constrain what options you can see. If you generate first, your options inform what criteria are relevant. The criteria-first approach may itself be a mechanism for premature closure — but no textbook discusses this.

Evaluation & Comparison — cross-textbook differences

  • Universal presence: Every step-sequence textbook devotes multiple chapters to evaluation. This is the methodologically richest part of every book.
  • Bardach: Two steps: "Project the outcomes" (predict what each option does) + "Confront the trade-offs" (compare across criteria). Emphasizes qualitative reasoning.
  • Weimer & Vining: Most extensive treatment: impact assessment, cost-benefit analysis, cost-effectiveness analysis, multi-goal analysis. Multiple chapters, ~120 pages.
  • Enserink/Delft: Ex-ante evaluation (multi-criteria analysis, CBA) + robustness analysis (sensitivity, scenario testing). Two distinct analytical phases.
  • Dunn: Forecasting (outcome projection) is a separate procedure from Evaluation (ex-post performance assessment). Note: Dunn's Monitoring and Evaluation are POST-implementation — they assess what happened, not what might happen.
  • Thissen & Walker: Explicitly separate "Assess alternatives" (per-option analysis) from "Compare alternatives" (cross-option ranking). The only textbook that formally distinguishes within-option from between-option evaluation.

The contrast with generation: Evaluation has named methods (CBA, MCA, sensitivity analysis), quantitative frameworks, software tools, and entire graduate courses. Generation has "brainstorm." The asymmetry is not accidental — it reflects what the field has invested in formalizing.

Decision & Communication — cross-textbook differences

  • Bardach: "Decide!" (one page) + "Tell your story" (a chapter on writing the policy memo). The decision itself gets almost no methodological treatment — it is treated as self-evident once the trade-offs are confronted.
  • Knoepfel: "Decision/adoption" is a political-process stage, not an analytical step. The analyst does not decide; the political system does.
  • Kingdon: The "policy window" — decisions happen when the three streams couple, often by chance. No methodology; the whole point is that decisions are contingent.
  • Cycle models (Dunn, Knoepfel): Include implementation and ex-post evaluation as additional stages after the decision. The rational-comprehensive models (Bardach) stop at the decision or the memo.

The split: Rational-comprehensive textbooks treat the decision as the endpoint of analysis. Cycle/process textbooks treat it as one stage in an ongoing loop. This affects what "option generation" means — is it a one-shot search, or a continuous process of policy learning?

Sequence Order — the hidden disagreement

The textbooks disagree on something they never discuss: when in the process should options be generated?

  • Generation-first (Bardach template): Problem → Evidence → Generate → Criteria → Evaluate. Used by: Bardach, Bromell, Thissen & Walker, Enserink.
  • Criteria-first: Problem → Criteria/Goals → Generate → Evaluate. Used by: Patton/Sawicki/Clark, Weimer & Vining, McLaughlin, Shalita & Schwartz.
  • Embedded in formulation: Agenda → Formulation (= generation + negotiation) → Decision. Used by: Knoepfel, Kingdon (descriptive).
  • Non-sequential: Five procedures applied iteratively, no fixed order. Used by: Dunn.

Why this matters for policy myopia: The criteria-first approach may constrain what options the analyst can see — you search within what your criteria make visible. The generation-first approach risks generating options without knowing what matters. Neither camp acknowledges the other's ordering as a choice with consequences.
