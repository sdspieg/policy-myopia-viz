The headline

Policy analysis textbooks have a developed methodology for choosing between options and an undeveloped one for generating them. The asymmetry is not that textbooks forget to mention alternatives — all 21 have a slot for it. It is that they treat generation as a checkbox while evaluation gets CBA, MCA, sensitivity testing, and entire graduate courses.

Five findings

1

The step exists; the method does not

All 21 textbooks include a generation slot. But only 4 provide named methods (Thissen & Walker ✦, Enserink/Delft ✦, Patton/Sawicki/Clark ✦, Nagel ✦). The other 17 say "brainstorm" or "consult practitioners." The evaluation side has CBA, MCA, sensitivity analysis, robustness testing — each with its own chapter.

2

The evaluation tail

Every analytical textbook follows generation with 2–4 evaluation steps. The median page ratio is roughly 4:1. Bardach: ~15pp generation vs ~50pp evaluation. Weimer & Vining: a subsection vs ~120pp. Only Bobrow & Dryzek invert this.

3

The criteria-first split

5 textbooks put criteria before generation. The rest follow Bardach's order. Neither camp discusses the other's ordering. If you set criteria first, you constrain what options you can see.

4

Formulation ≠ method

6 cycle-model textbooks have a "policy formulation" stage — but describe it as a political process, not an analytical method. Having a generation slot in a descriptive cycle is not having a generation method in a prescriptive toolkit.

5

One partial exception

Bobrow & Dryzek (1987) ✦✦ draw on 5 intellectual traditions for policy design. But their framework is multi-perspectival (different lenses on one problem), not cross-domain search (finding what another field did about a similar problem). The gap the policy myopia paper identifies goes further.

What the sequences reveal

1. Criteria-first vs generation-first. Bardach, Bromell, Thissen & Walker, Enserink, Mead, and Nagel put generation before criteria. But PSC, W&V, McLaughlin, S&S, and Mintrom reverse this.

2. The evaluation tail. Every analytical textbook follows generation with 2–4 red blocks. Cycle-model books go formulation→decision with no analytical evaluation in between.

Problem Definition

  • Bardach: Keep it small, actionable. ~10pp.
  • Weimer & Vining: 3 sub-steps with market/government failure frameworks.
  • Dunn: "Problem structuring" — boundary, classificational, hierarchy analysis. Most methodologically rich.
  • Enserink/Delft: Problem + system analysis + actor analysis. Multi-actor tradition.
  • Cycle models: "Agenda setting" — how problems reach the political agenda. Descriptive.
  • Bobrow & Dryzek: Problem framing through 5 intellectual traditions — the problem looks different from each.

Option Generation

  • Bardach: "Start with the obvious" + "ask practitioners." ~15pp. No method.
  • Weimer & Vining: A subsection. Lists sources, no protocol.
  • Patton/Sawicki/Clark ✦: Full chapter. No-action baseline, surveys, lit review, cross-jurisdictional comparison, policy-instrument typologies.
  • Thissen & Walker ✦: Separates generation from assessment. Scenario-based development. The only text treating generation as methodologically comparable to evaluation.
  • Enserink/Delft ✦: Scenarios + structured development. TU Delft tradition.
  • Nagel ✦: Analogy, decomposition, optimization as techniques. Pre-2000, largely forgotten.
  • Bobrow & Dryzek ✦✦: 5 intellectual traditions for option design. Multi-perspectival, not mechanism-transfer cross-domain search — but the closest any textbook comes.
  • Cycle models: "Formulation" describes WHERE options emerge politically. Not a method.
  • Everyone else: ~0.5–1ch. Brainstorm, consult, look at what others did.

The gap: Evaluation has CBA, MCA, sensitivity analysis — each a named method with its own chapter. Generation has "brainstorm." The step exists; the discipline does not.

Criteria Selection

  • Criteria-first (5 books): PSC, W&V, McLaughlin, S&S, Mintrom — know what you're looking for before you search.
  • Generation-first (6 books): Bardach, Bromell, T&W, Enserink, Mead, Nagel — generate then evaluate.
  • Bardach: Efficiency, equity, liberty, feasibility. A menu.
  • W&V: Client objectives + constraints.
  • Enserink/Delft: Criteria from actor analysis — different stakeholders, different criteria.

Why order matters: Criteria-first constrains what you can see. No textbook discusses this as a design choice.

Evaluation & Comparison

  • Universal: Every analytical textbook devotes multiple chapters. The richest part of every book.
  • Bardach: "Project outcomes" + "Confront trade-offs." Qualitative.
  • W&V: CBA, CEA, multi-goal analysis. ~120pp. Most extensive.
  • T&W: Formally separates "Assess" (per-option) from "Compare" (cross-option). Unique.
  • Cycle models: "Evaluation" is ex-post (did it work?) not ex-ante (which option is best?).

Decision & Communication

  • Bardach: "Decide!" (~1p) + "Tell your story" (~15pp). Decision gets almost no treatment.
  • Cycle models: Decision/adoption is political. The analyst doesn't decide.
  • B&D: "Contextual choice" — the right option depends on which frame you're in.

Sequence Order — the hidden disagreement

  • Generation-first: Bardach, Bromell, T&W, Enserink, Mead, Nagel.
  • Criteria-first: PSC, W&V, McLaughlin, S&S, Mintrom.
  • Embedded in formulation: Howlett, Kraft, Anderson, Pal, Birkland, Knoepfel, Kingdon.
  • Non-sequential: Dunn (iterative cycle).
  • Design-oriented: Bobrow & Dryzek.
