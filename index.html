The headline

Policy analysis has methods for generating options. The mainstream doesn't teach them. Six textbooks — out of the twenty-four with a step sequence — provide named methods for option generation: scenario-based design, morphological analysis, analogy, decomposition, cross-disciplinary framing. One (Bobrow & Dryzek 1987) organised an entire book around it. The methods exist. They have existed since the 1980s. The other eighteen textbooks — including Bardach, the most widely taught — say "brainstorm" and move on. The field's problem is not that nobody built a generation methodology. It is that the methodology keeps not being adopted.

Six findings

1

The methods exist — but only in a quarter of the textbooks

2

The component gradient

Option generation decomposes into 8 components (see the Option Generation tab). The left end — "survey existing policies," "include the status quo" — is commodity guidance present in the majority. The right end — scenario-based generation, cross-disciplinary design, generation as its own analytical phase — appears in only . The field invested in the easy end and left the hard end empty.

3

The evaluation tail

Every analytical textbook follows generation with 2–4 evaluation steps. The typical page ratio is roughly 3:1. Bardach: ~15pp generation vs ~50pp evaluation. Weimer & Vining: a subsection vs ~120pp. Only Bobrow & Dryzek invert this — because their entire book IS about generation.

4

The criteria-first split

textbooks put criteria before generation — the analyst is told to know what they're looking for before they search. The rest follow Bardach's order. Neither camp discusses the other's ordering as a design choice, and neither observes what the ordering implies for the scope of search.

5

Formulation ≠ method

6 cycle-model textbooks have a "policy formulation" stage — but describe it as a political process (what happens in government), not an analytical method (what an analyst should do). Having a generation slot in a descriptive cycle is not having a generation method in a prescriptive toolkit.

6

Cross-domain search: still missing

Even the strongest generation book — Bobrow & Dryzek — is multi-perspectival within the social sciences (welfare economics, public choice, sociology, information theory, political philosophy). It does not extend to ecology, engineering, or medicine, and it does not search for structural analogues across domains. The protocol the policy myopia paper proposes — "where has a structurally similar problem already been solved, in any field?" — goes further than any textbook in the corpus.

How this was coded — and where it may be wrong

Corpus. 27 policy-analysis textbooks from Zotero group 2366690, "Textbooks". 24 set out a step sequence and are compared step by step; 3 (Wildavsky & Peters, Fischer/Miller/Sidney, Morgan/Henrion/Small) have none and are listed but not sequenced. One book is non-English (Bobbio et al., IT); the rest are read in English editions. This is a textbook sample, not a survey of the field, and it is not a random one — it over-represents the Anglophone teaching canon.

Page counts are approximate (hence ~), taken from chapter and section boundaries rather than word counts, and are meant for order-of-magnitude comparison only. A 3:1 ratio here means "roughly three times", not a measured 3.0.

“Named method” (✦) means the book gives the analyst a procedure it names and can be followed — morphological analysis, scenario-based design, analogical transfer, decomposition, an instrument typology used generatively. General encouragement to “brainstorm”, “consult stakeholders” or “survey existing programs” does not count. Half-credit (0.5) marks a component mentioned but not developed, which is why the column totals are not whole numbers.

Who coded it. The initial coding was produced by LLM agents and put through two adversarial review rounds with two independent agents. It has not been verified by the authors of these textbooks, and it is the kind of judgement where a close reader may reasonably disagree — particularly on whether a given passage counts as a named method. Corrections are genuinely welcome and will be applied.

Sequence Order — the hidden disagreement

  • Generation-first: Bardach & Patashnik, Bromell, Mead, Nagel.
  • Criteria-first: Patton/Sawicki/Clark, Weimer & Vining, McLaughlin², Mintrom, Meltzer & Schwartz.
  • Generation step, no criteria step: Thissen & Walker, Enserink et al.
  • Embedded in formulation: Howlett/Ramesh/Perl, Kraft & Furlong, Anderson, Pal, Birkland, Knoepfel et al., Kingdon.
  • Non-sequential: Dunn (iterative cycle).
  • Design-oriented: Bobrow & Dryzek, Howlett (Designing).
  • Argument / framework, not a step model: Majone, Stone, Bobbio et al. (IT).
